# Choose a manageable portfolio

An attractive project is not automatically a worthwhile next commitment. Evaluate the projects together before recommending more work on any one of them. A useful result may be choosing one project and parking the rest, or protecting existing responsibilities and taking on no new commercial work.

## Establish the shared capacity

Use the user's local date to name the current and following calendar months explicitly. Do not hardcode the months or assume next month resembles this month. Ask only for missing facts, in small numbered batches:

- The maximum sustainable hours available for **all projects combined** in each month.
- For the current month, hours already used and the hours actually remaining. If past usage is unknown, a direct estimate of remaining capacity is enough; label it accordingly. Do not allocate a full month of work near the end of a month.
- Commitments **outside work** in each month and their impact: travel, family events, caregiving, exams, appointments, moving, rest, or other protected time. Exact private details are unnecessary. Clarify whether these are already deducted from the caps.
- Known support, maintenance, customer delivery, job-search, or other obligations. Clarify what is included in the project budget versus already excluded as protected time; count each once.

Treat a maximum as a ceiling, not a target to fill. Agree a realistic buffer or show an explicit provisional buffer assumption; do not silently assume either zero interruptions or a universal percentage. Check the available blocks and energy as well as total hours. A plan requiring weekday calls or frequent support can be incompatible with weekend-only availability even when its arithmetic fits.

If October has only a few days left, its remaining plan covers those days. November gets a separate plan using November's known commitments. Preserve this distinction in saved records and visuals. Weeks beyond the two-month horizon are conditional until capacity is revisited; do not roll the current cap forward as a promise. Refresh these facts at the next assessment or when circumstances change; do not create a scheduled check-in without a request.

## Compare all relevant projects

Use the central project list, current conversation, and the user's named projects to establish the active roster, including unpaid or personal projects consuming the same attention. Read the relevant expectations, commitments, progress, and evidence. Do not import unrelated project histories automatically. If records are incomplete, work with the supplied facts, mark gaps, and ask only what can change the ranking; missing records are not a reason to restart all assessments.

Compare the next meaningful outcome for each project against:

- The user's actual objective and time horizon, including income urgency, personal utility, or professional value.
- Evidence of demand, paying customers, repeat use, and access to buyers; distinguish observed traction from possible upside.
- Remaining work, demonstrated delivery pace, unfamiliar skills, and ongoing sales/support burden.
- Existing promises, deadlines, financial downside, and what the project would displace.
- Whether the user is willing and able to do the recurring work after the novelty wears off.

Use these reasons directly instead of a fabricated weighted score or success probability. Do not rank purely by excitement, code already written, market size, or the appeal of a new framework. A third-priority project with untested demand should not automatically receive a fresh validation sprint while higher-priority work is unfinished. Equally, do not always prefer the oldest project: stronger evidence can justify changing priorities.

Recommend **one priority by default and a second only when there is a clear reason and enough capacity**. Two is a maximum recommendation, not a quota. A hobby, OSS release, or “tiny experiment” still consumes hours and attention; do not use those labels to conceal additional active priorities. If the user chooses more, show the concrete tradeoffs and record their choice without presenting an overcommitted schedule as viable.

Give the remaining projects explicit treatment: maintenance only for obligations that cannot simply be dropped, or parked new work with a concrete reason and a revisit condition. Parking a commercial launch can leave a useful personal tool in use. Do not discontinue support owed to existing customers or change saved project status merely because the assistant recommends parking it.

## Allocate the actual months

For each month, show a compact allocation table: project, recommended mode, next outcome, allocated hours, and reason. Allocations must include the work needed to achieve that outcome: design decisions, learning, implementation, testing, recruitment, marketing, selling, delivery, and support where relevant. Avoid counting those items again in a separate overhead allowance.

Add project allocations and buffer, then compare that total with the hours available for the planning period. Show unused hours or the exact shortfall. Count shared work once, and include necessary maintenance for projects whose new development is parked. If only a range is known, show whether just the optimistic case fits; do not label the whole plan feasible on that basis.

When the plan exceeds capacity, reduce scope, sequence work, or park something. Do not solve the mismatch by borrowing from sleep, assuming a burst of motivation, extending every deadline indefinitely, or giving each project its own full weekly allowance. Preparing three individually reasonable experiments is not a portfolio decision. Choose the most useful next action from the combined set, including the option to do none.

A worthwhile experiment must earn its place against the alternatives. Specify its information value, cap, and decision it can change. If it is merely another route into building, repeated research, or polishing without confronting buyers, say so based on the observed pattern. Do not assume avoidance from a single technical task.

## Be candid about outcomes

Separate three judgments: the problem may be real, this product may be buildable, and investment in this product may be justified **for this user now**. Say which is supported. “Could make money” is too weak to serve as a recommendation; most speculative ideas satisfy it.

When commercial evidence is missing, state that plainly. Estimate the time to prepare or execute a test only where justified; its completion does not imply a first payment. Present an income target and any hypothetical acquisition scenario as a target and scenario, not a likely date of success. Include a plausible low- or no-sales case when recommending meaningful investment, along with the time, cash, and obligations the user would still incur.

Lead with the recommendation and its strongest reasons. Keep detail available through compact tables or optional visuals rather than burying the verdict under an encouraging business plan. End with one next action or a deliberate pause, plus a clear review point. Ask only the numbered questions that could materially change the decision.
