# Market and demand research

Use this reference for Be Real's commercialization research. The objective is a defensible decision about the proposed product for this builder, with their constraints. Scale the research to the decision; a small paid experiment does not need an exhaustive industry report.

## Frame the question and gather evidence

Establish the customer, payer, problem, geography, proposed offer, and what a worthwhile financial outcome means. Use existing context and ask only what materially affects the research. If a segment is still hypothetical, state it and compare plausible segments rather than silently choosing one.

Browse the internet for current evidence. Search both the proposed solution and the problem in customers' language. Include direct competitors, adjacent products, services, spreadsheets or manual workarounds, and doing nothing. Search for prior launches and abandoned attempts as well as successful examples. Look for evidence against the idea, not only evidence supporting it.

Choose sources for the claim being tested:

- Use actual product, pricing, documentation, and launch pages to establish what alternatives offer and when. Marketing claims about adoption or superiority remain claims unless corroborated.
- Use public statistics, credible industry datasets, filings, or documented buyer populations for market counts. Check definitions, geography, publication date, and the period measured. Do not use a large report headline as a substitute for the relevant segment.
- Use customer reviews, forums, requests, community discussions, and documented workflows to understand pain and alternatives. Note recency, buyer fit, selection bias, and whether several pages repeat the same underlying evidence. A complaint is not automatically willingness to pay.
- Prefer observed purchases, renewals, sustained use, or meaningful commitments when available. Search interest, traffic estimates, waitlists, upvotes, and social enthusiasm are proxies; they do not directly measure paying customers. Relative search-trend indices are not absolute search volumes.

Record source links and dates for material findings. Open sources that support central claims instead of relying only on search snippets. Triangulate consequential claims where possible and explain conflicts. Do not claim access to paywalled details, private sales data, exact search volumes, or conversion rates you cannot verify.

Stop the initial research pass when the major decision questions have useful evidence or a clearly identified gap and further searches are repeating findings. Name what remains unresolved and the next check that could resolve it. Keep internet research distinct from customer validation; proposing an interview, ad test, or pilot does not authorize contacting people, spending money, or publishing anything.

## Is there a real need?

Identify the specific job customers need done, how often it occurs, the cost or frustration of the current approach, and who has the authority and budget to buy. Separate an interesting feature from an urgent problem. Look for existing spending or costly workarounds and the circumstances that would trigger a purchase.

Give a qualified verdict on the need and on paid demand separately. Explain the strongest supporting evidence, the strongest counterevidence, and what is still untested. Weak public evidence means uncertainty, not proof that demand is absent. Existing competitors can support the demand case without proving that another entrant can acquire customers profitably.

## Answer the market questions directly

### Is it unique, and are we first to market?

Compare the promised outcome, buyer segment, workflow, delivery model, price, and distribution with direct and indirect alternatives. Distinguish a novel feature, a differentiated offer, and a defensible advantage. Using AI or combining familiar features does not by itself establish any of these.

Check dated launches and earlier attempts when making a novelty claim. Scope the result to the segment and research performed: “No close equivalent found in this search” is supportable when true; “first ever” generally is not. Explain whether being early helps or creates education and adoption costs. Uniqueness is neither necessary nor sufficient for a viable business.

### How large could the market be?

Prefer a bottom-up range: relevant buyers or accounts multiplied by a plausible annual spend. Show the source or explicit assumption for each input, units, geography, year, eligibility filters, and overlap. Avoid counting users and paying organizations as if they were interchangeable.

Separate the broad addressable market, the segment this offer can serve, and the customers this builder could plausibly reach over a stated horizon. Estimate reach from channels and capacity, not an arbitrary “1% of the market.” Test whether the reachable segment could support the user's income goal. If credible counts or spending data are unavailable, state that a numerical market estimate is not yet defensible and show the customer threshold the business would need to meet.

### Can this product compete?

Use a compact comparison of the meaningful alternatives: target buyer, offer and price, strengths, relevant gaps, switching barriers, and sources. Distinguish an observed gap from a hypothesized one. Include the customer's current workaround, even when it costs little cash.

Research the commercial state of each meaningful competitor, not just its feature set. Look for revenue, recurring revenue, paying customers, adoption, growth or decline, and market share where available. Describe its stage only as far as the evidence supports, such as pre-revenue, early paid adoption, or established commercial traction; otherwise mark the stage unknown. A polished product or prominent launch does not establish a successful business.

For material traction figures, show the metric and value, product or company scope, measurement period, source link and publication date, and evidence status. Distinguish documented financial disclosures, company or founder claims, third-party estimates, your own calculations, and unavailable data. Prefer audited financial statements and relevant filings for financial results, and dated primary disclosures for other metrics. Check that any audited status actually covers the metric cited. A founder interview or revenue screenshot is attributed evidence, not independent verification; repeated articles quoting the same claim are not separate corroboration. For estimates, inspect the method and present a range when supported rather than false precision. Resolve conflicting figures where possible and retain uncertainty when not.

Keep comparisons financially and statistically consistent:

- Distinguish recognized revenue, ARR or MRR, bookings, transaction volume, and profit. Do not substitute funding or valuation for revenue, or parent-company revenue for the competing product's revenue. Do not infer paying customers from registrations, downloads, visitors, or customer logos.
- A market-share claim needs a defined market, geography, period, metric, and denominator. Revenue share, customer share, usage share, and share of web traffic are different measures. Calculate a share only when the numerator and denominator are credibly sourced and comparable; label it as a calculation with its inputs. Otherwise report market share as unknown. Do not manufacture shares from an arbitrary competitor list or a broad industry market estimate.
- Use the latest credible figures found, but state the period they describe. An old revenue milestone does not establish current revenue or current growth. Private-company non-disclosure means unknown, not zero or failure.

Explain what this evidence changes: it may support paid demand, reveal incumbent resources or distribution strength, or show that apparent competitors have unproven traction. Revenue alone does not prove profitability, defensibility, or that this builder can win. Carry these limits into the competitive assessment and the questions posed to the user.

Treat newly found competitors as a provisional shortlist for discussion. For each meaningful candidate, link the actual product and explain why the same buyer might choose it instead: overlap in the customer problem, outcome, budget, or workflow. Note differences and classify it as a direct competitor, indirect substitute, or adjacent product rather than equating similar keywords with competition.

Ask the user to check the shortlist: “Would your intended customers actually compare your product with these? What am I missing?” Also ask: “Knowing these alternatives exist, is there a reason you believe you can still compete—such as a customer insight, an unmet need, access to buyers, or a better workflow?” Invite corrections without requiring the user to defend the idea. Show evidence and reasoning before asking; do not hand the research work back to them.

Incorporate their answers into the comparison and recommendation. Distinguish the user's intended positioning from independently verified market facts, and check consequential claims about competitors where possible. If the user disagrees, explain any remaining evidence-based concern respectfully; neither automatically dismiss a competitor nor override a useful correction. If they have not responded, keep classifications and dependent conclusions provisional while continuing independent work. Do not ask them to re-review competitors already discussed unless new evidence changes the comparison.

Assess a specific entry opportunity: an underserved segment, easier workflow, better outcome, trusted access, distribution advantage, or another reason a buyer would switch. Consider incumbent trust, integrations, existing contracts, customer acquisition, and how easily the advantage could be copied. Account for the builder's domain knowledge, audience, budget, and available time. Give a conditional judgment with reasons, not an invented probability of success or a claim that feature parity will win.

### How difficult is go-to-market?

Identify plausible ways to reach the first paying customers and the route to repeatable acquisition. For each promising channel, explain where suitable buyers are, how the builder can reach them, what offer they would see, the sales steps, likely barriers, cash cost assumptions, and human work required. Evaluate actual channel access; “post on social media,” “run ads,” or “do SEO” is not a complete plan.

Consider audience-building time, existing relationships, proof or trust requirements, trials, onboarding, switching friction, buying cycles, procurement, and retention where relevant. Assess difficulty through these concrete constraints rather than an unexplained score. Distinguish a few labor-intensive founder sales from a repeatable channel.

For a numerical acquisition scenario, make the funnel visible: reachable qualified prospects, response or engagement, evaluation, paid conversion, elapsed sales cycle, and retained customers. Label unmeasured rates as assumptions and show sensitivity rather than importing universal benchmarks. Acquisition cost includes relevant cash spending and human effort, reported separately. Check whether calls, demos, and support fit the builder's other commitments. Limit projected growth to what the channel, budget, and operating capacity can support.

Estimate the user's distribution work using their demonstrated proficiency in the specific activity, following [progress-estimation.md](progress-estimation.md). Ask about prior marketing, sales, onboarding, or support outcomes rather than borrowing their coding pace. Include learning, practice, and feedback where unfamiliar work is required; increased proficiency alone does not establish demand or guarantee conversion.

### How long and how hard until it is worth the money and effort?

Translate “worth it” into the user's goal. Distinguish four milestones when relevant: first sale, covering recurring operating costs, recovering upfront investment, and earning the desired income or return on time. These can occur at very different times; some may never be reached within the planning horizon.

Build a simple conditional model using a consistent period and currency:

- Revenue depends on paying customers, price, and actual billing frequency. For subscriptions, model additions and churn; for one-off sales, model new transactions rather than assuming recurring revenue. Treat acquisition and retention figures as scenarios until observed.
- Contribution per customer or sale equals revenue less variable delivery costs, including relevant API usage, hosting, fees, refunds, and paid fulfillment. If contribution is zero or negative, more customers do not solve the problem under those assumptions.
- Period operating cash surplus subtracts fixed operating costs and acquisition cash spending from total contribution. Include setup costs in the upfront investment and avoid counting the same cost twice. Show how the required customer count changes with pricing, delivery cost, and acquisition assumptions.
- Cash recovery occurs only when cumulative operating cash surplus covers the stated upfront cash investment. Use the acquisition ramp, sales-cycle delays, and retention assumptions to estimate a range; dividing an investment by hypothetical mature monthly profit is not a credible launch timeline.
- Track founder hours for research, building, selling, and ongoing delivery. Show cash results separately from an economic view that values those hours at an explicitly agreed or hypothetical rate. State whether owner compensation is already counted so labor is not charged twice. Past spending may matter to the user's recovery goal, but it does not by itself justify further investment.

Use a small set of transparent scenarios, including an adverse case with slow or no sales where plausible. Show which inputs dominate the outcome and whether the required sales and support effort fit the user's schedule. Do not give a precise payback date when demand, acquisition, or pricing is unresolved. Explain what evidence would make the range more useful.

## Synthesize the decision

Lead with whether the evidence supports a worthwhile next experiment or commitment. Answer need, uniqueness, first-to-market status, market size, competitive opportunity, GTM difficulty, and economics at the depth the user requested. A compact table of judgment, evidence, and uncertainty often suffices; link sources beside claims and state the research date and scope. Avoid a link dump or a generic business plan.

Separate researched facts from calculations and assumptions. Carry the findings into the scope, workload, calendar, and recommendation in SKILL.md. Identify the most consequential unanswered question. Recommend a bounded validation step only if it earns a priority slot and fits the shared monthly budget under [portfolio-planning.md](portfolio-planning.md); otherwise recommend deferring or parking it. Give any selected test a success criterion, effort or spending cap, and a reason to continue, change direction, or stop. Weak evidence does not automatically justify another experiment, and time to complete a test is not a forecast of the first payment.
